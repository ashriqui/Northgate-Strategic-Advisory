The London Factory Floor

Where a $2 Billion Contract Goes

Defence Procurement Supply Chain Small Business

Big contracts are just a lot of small ones, wearing a trench coat.

On July 16th, Mark Carney toured a General Dynamics plant in London, Ontario, then stood in front of a camera and announced nearly $2 billion for 190 armoured combat support vehicles. He did not build any of them. Nobody in that room did. That's worth sitting with before you skip past another headline about a defence contract too big to mean anything to you.

A finished armoured vehicle isn't built in London so much as it arrives there. Steel gets shaped in one province, electronics get assembled in another, some contractor a thousand kilometers away who has never once stood next to the prime minister finishes a part, boxes it, and ships it to a factory floor where somebody else bolts it into place. Carney said as much himself, crediting an entire supply chain of Canadian ingenuity, skill and craftsmanship behind every vehicle. GDLS-Canada draws on more than 600 Canadian suppliers spread across over 100 communities to make that true.

Four of those suppliers got named specifically: Ryan Manufacturing in Richmond, Interpro in Regina, Thales Canada in Saint-Laurent, IMP Aerospace and Defence in Enfield. None of them are household names outside their own towns. All of them just had a very good year, and earned it long before Carney ever showed up to shake a hand.

This isn't the program's first pass at spreading the work around, either. In 2020, the last time this vehicle line went into production, General Dynamics handed out more than $137 million in subcontracts to over 30 Canadian businesses before a single finished vehicle existed. The prime hasn't changed since. The number has, again, gotten bigger, and it will move through the country the same way it did last time, quietly, in pieces, to companies that were ready when the phone rang.

That's the real opportunity in an announcement like this, and it has nothing to do with building armoured vehicles. Ottawa's defence buildup runs on primes who are contractually obligated to spread the work around the country, which means the door in has very little to do with how big you are. Nobody needs to win the $2 billion contract. They need to be findable, certified, and standing ready when a prime contractor goes looking for its hundredth supplier, not just its first.

The vehicles rolling out of London will get all the attention, because they're the part you can put a soldier in and photograph. But the ones earning something new from this deal are the businesses nobody in Ottawa has heard of yet, quietly getting the call, one subcontract at a time.
